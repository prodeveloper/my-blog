I've been reading Eben Hewitt's argument pattern lately, and it's one of those chapters that quietly changes how you hear every meeting you sit in afterward. It's about the three elements of a strong argument and the fallacies that quietly sink you if you're not paying attention.

The three appeals are older than the tech industry by a couple thousand years. Aristotle named them: the **logical, the ethical, and the pathetic**. That last one sounds alarming, but it just means emotional. You appeal to the audience's reason, to their trust in you, and to their feelings. The strongest arguments touch all three at once.

## The three appeals in practice

For years I leaned almost entirely on logic. I assumed that if the facts were right and the reasoning was sound, the room would come around. It doesn't work that way. A slide full of correct numbers lands flat if no one trusts you and no one feels the stakes.

**Logical appeal** is the argument itself, the evidence and the chain of reasoning. It's necessary but not sufficient.

**Ethical appeal** is why anyone should believe you. It's your track record, your honesty about what you don't know, your willingness to admit when a plan could fail. You don't get to state this one. You earn it over time, and the room either senses it or not.

**Pathetic appeal** is the emotional weight. Not manipulation, not a sob story. It's making people feel the cost of doing nothing and the opportunity of acting now. For a compliance audience, that's not fear-mongering. It's surfacing the very real exposure a team has been quietly ignoring.

## The fallacies that sink you

The chapter also walks through the common logical fallacies, and reading them was uncomfortable because I've committed most of them at some point.

The one that shows up most in tech is **post hoc, ergo propter hoc**, Latin for "after this, therefore because of this." We do it constantly in troubleshooting. You upgrade Java, an hour later the server goes down, therefore the upgrade caused the outage. At 2am on a critical incident call, that conclusion is magnetic. It feels like progress.

Here's the thing. All you've actually done is identify a candidate to investigate. That's a necessary step. It's just not a conclusion. The upgrade is a suspect, not a verdict. If you treat it like a verdict, you waste the incident chasing the wrong cause while the real one keeps running.

The other common one is when someone restates their premise as if it were evidence. You ask them to back a claim, and they just say the claim again with more confidence. The way to break that loop is to ask for evidence, specifically. Make them show their work.

## Where this bites in my world

The compliance work I've been doing is one long exercise in persuasion. Drafting a CAPA, writing an incident communications procedure, building a risk register: none of it ships on its own. It ships because someone in a decision-making meeting agrees to advance it.

The temptation is to treat these as pure logic problems. Here are the gaps, here are the controls, here's the evidence, therefore approve. It should work. It doesn't always.

What actually moves the room is the mix. Show the control gaps and the evidence, absolutely. But also let them feel what a failed audit or an unrecorded incident actually costs. And over months, build the credibility that means when you flag an exposure, people take it seriously before they see the evidence, because they've learned to trust your read.

## The line before the big meeting

One habit I've stolen from this pattern is a mental check before any important presentation. I ask myself: did I bring the logic, the credibility, and the emotional weight, or did I just bring a deck?

If all I have is a deck, I'm presenting to a cold audience with one hand tied. If I've done the persuasion work in advance, talked to the stakeholders individually, and earned their trust, then the presentation is just the formal close of a conversation that already happened.

Logic wins the arguments that are already winnable. Trust and momentum win the ones that aren't.
