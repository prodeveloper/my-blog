I've been reading Eben Hewitt's *Technology Strategy Patterns*, and one short aside has stuck with me more than the chapters around it. He compares strategic analysis to machine learning. It sounds like a gimmick until you sit with it.

## What the machine is learning

Most people picture machine learning as a machine learning what to do. That isn't quite it. **What the machine learns is a function: the one that best explains the data.** You give it inputs, it searches for the `f` in `Y = f(x)` that draws the cleanest line through the points, and then it uses that line to label things it has never seen.

The process runs in five steps:

1. Decide the question, the label you want to predict.
2. Find data that can answer it, then clean it and fill the gaps.
3. Choose a model.
4. Fit it.
5. Predict.

Hewitt's point is that a strategist does the same thing. You aren't collecting facts for their own sake. **You're looking for the mental model that explains what you see well enough to say what happens next.**

## Where I see it in my own week

Picture a kitchen where the bins keep overflowing. One person says the bins are too small. Another says people aren't emptying them. A third says the shopping has changed and there's more packaging now. Each of those is a model. Each one fits some of the points.

The trap is picking the first model that fits and stopping. I do this more than I'd like. A recurring problem shows up at work, I recognise a shape I've seen before, and I reach for the old fix. Sometimes it works. Often the line I drew only fit last month's data.

**Holding more than one hypothesis early is the cheapest insurance against a confident wrong answer.** Hewitt says it plainly in his summary: gather data quickly to form more than one hypothesis, then narrow, assign probabilities, and go back for more data.

## Analysis is a fractal

The idea I liked most is that this process is fractal. The same pattern works at every scale. For a big decision you do it formally, with research, documents and discussion. For a small claim someone makes in a meeting, you run it in your head in ten seconds:

- What's the real question?
- What would I need to see to believe this?
- What other explanation fits the same facts?
- How likely is each?

**Once it becomes a habit, you stop needing a workshop to think clearly.** The formal version and the informal version are the same tool at different zoom levels.

## Separate the problem from the solution

One line near the end is easy to skim past: when you're handed a problem, analyse the problem and the solution separately.

People rarely hand you a problem. They hand you a problem with a solution already attached. "We need a new tool for this." The tool might be right, but if you only evaluate the tool, you've skipped the step where you check whether the line you're fitting is the right line at all.

**A good solution to a misread problem is still a miss.**

## Remember

- Strategy, like machine learning, is the search for a model that explains the data and predicts what comes next.
- Start with more than one hypothesis.
- Use the same process at every scale, from a board paper to a passing remark.
- Pull the problem and the proposed solution apart before judging either.

The world keeps producing new data points. The job is to keep checking whether your line still fits them.
