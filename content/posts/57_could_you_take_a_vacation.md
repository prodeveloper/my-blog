I have a test I run on myself as a manager. **If I disappeared for two weeks with no phone, would the work keep moving?** Not perfectly. Just moving.

The answer tells me more about my team than any dashboard does. Lately I have been reading the leadership chapters of *Software Engineering at Google*, and one line from the section on antipatterns stuck with me: if you build a team of pushovers, you probably can't take a vacation, because the moment you leave the room, productivity comes to a screeching halt.

## The Quiet Way Work Drifts Upward

Nobody sets out to build a team that depends on them. It happens one helpful act at a time.

An engineer is stuck on a ticket. I know the answer, so I write it in the thread. Next week someone asks a similar question, and they ask me directly instead of reading the code. A month later, I am drafting the technical reply to a partner team because "it's faster if you do it."

Each step felt like support. Together they moved the work from the people who own it to me. I caught myself recently writing a follow up message that explained, in detail, what an engineer should do next on their own ticket. A good message to send would have been one line: **"What is our plan here?"** The engineer owns the answer. My job is to ask the question that moves it.

## Treat People Like Children and They Will Act Like It

The book makes a point that sounds obvious until you notice yourself breaking it: people tend to act the way you treat them. If you check every step, people stop taking steps without you. If you rewrite every reply, people stop writing replies.

Think of a kitchen where the head chef tastes every plate before it leaves the pass. At first it feels like quality control. After a while, the line cooks stop tasting their own food. Why would they? Someone else will catch it. **The chef has trained the team to outsource their judgment**, and now the chef cannot leave the pass for a minute.

The fix is not to stop caring about quality. It is to hand back the tasting spoon.

## Hire People Who Can Replace You

The book's advice is to hire people smarter than you who could do your job. That is uncomfortable, because those same people will challenge you and point out your mistakes. They will also direct themselves, and some will want to lead.

That is the point. **A team that can run without you frees you to do the work only you can do**: looking further ahead, clearing obstacles between teams, and growing the next lead.

## Failure Has to Be Allowed

None of this works if people are afraid to get it wrong. If an engineer makes a call while I am away and it goes badly, my reaction decides whether they ever make a call again.

The book is clear on this. Praise individuals in public. Give criticism in private. When something breaks, run a blameless review that looks for the cause and fixes it once, instead of looking for someone to point at. **Fast, small failures are cheap lessons.** The expensive failure is the team that never tries anything because the last person who tried got blamed.

## What I Am Changing

A few habits I am working on:

- When I am asked a question I could answer, I first ask what the person thinks.
- My follow ups ask one question that moves the work: status, date, blocker, or plan. No instructions.
- When someone makes a call I would have made differently, I let it stand unless the cost is real.
- I keep a short list of decisions only I am making, and try to hand one of them off each month.

## Remember

The measure of a lead is not how much passes through their hands. **It is how little stops when they step away.** If you could not take a vacation tomorrow, the problem is not your workload. It is how the team was built, and that is something you can change.
