I've been reading *Software Engineering at Google*, and one image from the chapter on leading teams has stuck with me all week. Picture the organisation as a chain of gears. The person doing the work is a small gear at one end. Each leader above them is a bigger gear. **When a big gear turns once, the small gears at the end spin several times.**

You don't have to mean it. You just have to move.

## A Small Turn Upstairs

Think of a kitchen during dinner service. The head chef glances at a plate and frowns for half a second. She's thinking about the supplier, not the food. But the line cook saw the frown. Now he's remaking a dish that was fine, the next order is late, and two other cooks are watching him and wondering what went wrong.

I see the same thing in my own week. A quick "why is this still open?" in a chat, typed between meetings, can land as an alarm on someone else's desk. **The weight of a message comes from who sends it, not from how much thought went into it.**

The book puts it plainly: the leader is always on stage. Not only in the big meeting, but in how you react to small talk and what your face does when a plan slips.

## Calm Is a Tool

The authors tell a story about a manager who never panicked. When something blew up, he would rest his chin on his hand and ask questions. The panicked engineer in front of him would slow down and start thinking again.

That isn't a personality trait you either have or don't. It's a choice you make in the first ten seconds. **Your team borrows your reaction before they form their own.** If I meet a setback with curiosity, the people around me tend to do the same. If I meet it with alarm, I've doubled the problem.

This doesn't mean pretending things are fine. It means letting people know you see the obstacles without spinning them up about it.

## Ask, Don't Fix

The second trick in the chapter is harder for me. When someone brings a problem, my instinct is to solve it. That was my job for years, and it feels good.

But most people asking for advice don't want the answer handed over. They want help finding it. A few questions do more than a solution:

- What have you tried so far?
- What would you do if I weren't here?
- What's the part that worries you most?

**When I solve it for them, I've fixed one problem. When I help them solve it, I've fixed the next ten.**

## Say Sorry Properly

The chapter also covers something engineers avoid: apologising when you get it wrong. Your team already knows you made the mistake. They've probably discussed it. Owning it costs nothing and earns a lot, because it shows you can assess a situation clearly, including your own part in it.

## Remember

Every leader is a bigger gear, whether they feel like one or not. Move slowly, stay calm, ask before you answer, and own your mistakes. **The smallest gears feel every turn you make.**
