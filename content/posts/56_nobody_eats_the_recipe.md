I spend a good part of my week on work that no customer will ever see. Policies, registers, evidence for reviews, plans for plans. All of it matters. None of it is the thing we sell.

Eben Hewitt's *Technology Strategy Patterns* gave me a clean way to think about this, and a warning I needed to hear.

## Deliverables, Outputs, Outcomes

Hewitt separates three things that most teams blur together:

- **Deliverable**: a concrete piece of work that has value only inside your process. A project plan, an architecture document, a release checklist.
- **Output**: what a customer can see, use and pay for. The product or the service.
- **Outcome**: the difference that output makes for the customer. Lower costs, faster delivery, better reliability.

His line on deliverables stuck with me: **they are a means, not an end. On their own, no one cares.**

Think of a restaurant kitchen. The recipe card is a deliverable. The plate is the output. The outcome is a guest who leaves happy and comes back next week. Nobody eats the recipe. A kitchen can have beautiful, laminated, version-controlled recipes and still send out cold food.

## The Support Team Trap

Hewitt splits departments into two kinds. **Value creators** make the things the company sells. **Support** functions (finance, legal, HR, infrastructure, governance) exist to make the value creators faster, safer and compliant.

Then he describes what happens when a company forgets the difference: support teams start acting like the value creators exist to feed their processes. The form becomes the point. Engineers become suppliers of evidence.

I take this personally, because a lot of my week now sits on the support side. When I keep a register of open findings or write a policy for sign off, I have to ask myself one question: **who is faster, safer or calmer because this exists?** If I can't name them, I've written a recipe nobody will cook.

## Working From the Outside In

The method Hewitt lays out starts with the customer and works backward:

1. Name the outcome the customer wants.
2. Name the output that delivers it.
3. Only then list the deliverables and activities needed to produce that output.

He deliberately ignores deliverables until the end. **If you start from the inside, you optimize your own paperwork. If you start from the outside, the paperwork earns its place or disappears.**

I've started applying this to my own recurring work. A weekly status report is a deliverable. The output is a decision someone can make on Monday morning. The outcome is fewer surprises for the people building the product. When I frame it that way, half the report turns out to be noise, and the useful half gets sharper.

## Principles Someone Could Argue With

One more idea from the chapter I keep coming back to. A principle is only useful if a reasonable person could argue the opposite. "Automation" is a real principle, because "manual" is a real alternative with real tradeoffs. "Quality" is not, because nobody argues for poor quality.

The same test works for support work. "We keep good records" says nothing. "We record decisions where the engineers already work, not in a separate system" is a choice. Someone could disagree. That's what makes it useful.

## Remember

Deliverables are how the work gets done. Outputs are what people receive. Outcomes are why anyone cares. **If you work in a support role, your job is to make someone else's output better, and the moment your process becomes the point, you've stopped cooking and started laminating recipes.**
