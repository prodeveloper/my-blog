I make a lot of lists. Status lists, risk lists, lists of things waiting on someone else. Most mornings start with one. For a long time I treated them as a record of the world. Eben Hewitt's *Technology Strategy Patterns* changed how I see them: **a list is not a record, it is the raw material of a decision.**

Hewitt goes further than I expected. He says the single most important thing you can do to improve your strategy is to get good at making lists. Your strategy, in his words, is a list of lists. If the lists are weak, everything built on them crumbles.

## Who Is This List For?

Hewitt's first test is simple. Before you worry about what goes on the list, be clear on two things:

- **Who the audience is**
- **Why they care**

He sharpens that with two questions. After reading this list, can someone make a decision they could not make before? Can they go and do something they would not have known to do?

If the answer to both is no, the list is decoration. It helps people "understand", and Hewitt has little patience for that. **A list that only informs is a list nobody will act on.**

I recognised myself in this. I keep a weekly list of open items that need chasing. It used to be a long column of everything still outstanding. People nodded at it and moved on. When I rewrote it so each line said who owns the next step and what they need to decide, the same items started moving.

## Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive

The second test is MECE, pronounced "mee-see". Every item on the list should be:

1. **Mutually exclusive**: no two items overlap.
2. **Collectively exhaustive**: together they cover the whole thing, with nothing left out.

The examples in the book are clean. The four suits in a deck of cards. The four seasons. Profit equals revenue minus cost. That last one is my favourite, because it reminds you there are only two ways to grow profit. **If your list of options only talks about cutting cost, you have quietly forgotten half the problem.**

## What Breaks When a List Is Not MECE

Think about a household budget. Say your categories are "Food", "Groceries", "Eating Out" and "Bills". Groceries and eating out both sit inside food, so some spending gets counted twice. Meanwhile there is no line for the car, so that money vanishes. You look at the totals and make a confident decision on numbers that are wrong in both directions.

Work lists fail the same way:

- **Overlap** means two people think they own the same thing, or each assumes the other does.
- **Gaps** mean something important belongs to nobody, and it surfaces later as a surprise.

When I review a tracker now, I ask two questions of it. Could any item sit in two places? Is there anything happening that has no home here? The second one is the harder question, and the more useful.

## Lean Lists

Hewitt's word for good lists is lean. A lean list points toward work someone will do, or gives a decision maker what they need to choose. A RACI is a good example: if your name is next to an item with an "R", you know to go and do it.

The test I use now before sending any list is short:

- Who is reading this?
- What will they decide or do after reading it?
- Does anything overlap?
- Is anything missing?

## Remember

Strategy sounds grand, but most of it is built from ordinary lists. **Know who the list is for, make it lead to a decision, and make sure every piece fits exactly once.** Get that right and the bigger work gets easier.
