I've been reading *Technology Strategy Patterns* by Eben Hewitt, and one short story in the chapter on the value chain stopped me cold.

Hewitt pitched an automation idea to his company president, a former CFO. He told her it would save half a million dollars, because work done by ten people could be done by six. She asked one question: **"Which four people are you going to fire today?"**

He didn't want to fire anyone. So she told him to fix his deck. If the people are still on the payroll, it isn't cost savings.

## The Claim I Keep Making

I recognise myself in that story. Over the past few months I've automated a fair amount of my own work: scheduled jobs that refresh dashboards overnight, scripts that sweep documents for stale numbers, checks that catch a mistake before a human reviewer does.

When I describe this work, the easy sentence is "this saves me hours a week." It sounds like money. It isn't. **Nobody's budget got smaller because my Tuesday got lighter.**

What did happen is more interesting:

- Mistakes that used to reach a reviewer now get caught by a script first.
- Work that waited for me to remember it now happens on a schedule.
- The hours I got back went into problems that had been sitting untouched.

Those are real benefits. They're just not the benefit I was tempted to claim.

## Three Kinds of Value

Hewitt splits technology value into three buckets, and they help me sort my own claims:

1. **Sustain the value.** Keep the lights on. Nobody notices until it breaks.
2. **Maximize the value.** Do the same things better: fewer errors, faster turnaround.
3. **Discover value.** Find something new that wasn't possible before.

Most automation lives in the second bucket. **Better quality and faster turnaround are a strong pitch on their own.** Dressing them up as headcount reduction makes the whole pitch weaker, because the first sharp question exposes it.

## A Kitchen Example

Picture a restaurant that buys a dishwasher. The owner says it will save the cost of one kitchen porter. But the porter stays on, now prepping vegetables instead of scrubbing pans. The wage bill is the same.

So what did the dishwasher buy? Faster table turns. Cleaner plates. A cook who no longer waits for a clean pan during the rush. **That's capacity, and capacity is only worth what you choose to do with it.**

If the owner never puts the porter on prep, the dishwasher bought nothing but a quieter sink.

## The Questions I Now Ask

Before I describe any improvement, I run it past a short list:

- If this is cost savings, which line on which budget goes down, and when?
- If it's time saved, what will that time be spent on instead?
- If it's quality, what failure stops happening, and how would anyone notice?
- Am I keeping a step because it adds value, or because "that's how we've always done it"?

That last one comes straight from Hewitt's efficiency list, and it cuts both ways. Some of my own automations exist to prop up processes that should simply be dropped.

## Remember

**Name the benefit you can defend.** Saved time is capacity, not cash, until someone decides what to do with it. A smaller, true claim survives the first hard question. A big, loose one doesn't, and it takes your credibility with it.
