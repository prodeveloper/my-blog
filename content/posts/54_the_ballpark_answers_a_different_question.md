Someone asks me how much a piece of work will cost. My instinct is to go away and come back with a precise number. Line items, contingency, a neat total. It feels responsible.

I have been reading Eben Hewitt's *Technology Strategy Patterns*, and one chapter changed how I hear that question. **When a leader asks for an estimate, they are rarely asking what it costs. They are asking whether to do it at all.**

## The question behind the question

Hewitt calls the pattern Directional Costing. The point is simple: the person asking wants to know if the thing is more like one unit of money or ten. They are not planning the budget yet. They are comparing your option against others you may not even know about.

His example stuck with me. A team estimates a two year build. Meanwhile the executive is quietly wondering whether they could buy a whole company that already does this, get it today, and inherit its people and customers. Your careful estimate is one input into a choice between building, buying and partnering. **If you take three weeks to be precise, the decision gets made without you.**

## Third grade math is enough

The method is almost embarrassing in its simplicity. Pick a standard team shape. Multiply people by a blended rate by hours by sprint length. That gives you the cost of one sprint. Then work out how many teams the work can keep busy in parallel and how many sprints it needs. Multiply. Add a slice for the part time supporting roles. Done.

The part I like most is the order of work. **Put the showstoppers first.** If something could sink the project, find out early, before the spending piles up. You design the project just enough to know how many teams can run side by side, and no further.

## Write the assumptions down

A rough number with no assumptions attached is a trap. Someone remembers the number and forgets the conditions. Six months later the direction changes, the cost changes, and you look careless.

Hewitt's fix is to list the things that could change and that your number leans on. I think of it like a household budget for a renovation. "This assumes we keep the existing plumbing" is the sentence that saves the argument later when someone decides to move the kitchen.

I have been building a few small cost views lately, the kind that show where money goes each month. The lesson that keeps coming back is the same: **the number is only trusted when the reasoning sits right next to it.** People can argue with an assumption. They cannot argue with a bare total, so they just stop believing it.

## Make it a template

The other habit worth stealing: every time you are asked for an estimate, leave behind a reusable template. A summary page for the slide, the detail and assumptions underneath, and cells that recalculate when an assumption moves. The second estimate takes minutes. The tenth one makes you the person leaders call first.

## What I am changing

Next time someone asks me what something will cost, I will answer with a range, the three assumptions it rests on, and the one thing that could kill it. Then I will ask what they are weighing it against. **A fast, labelled ballpark beats a perfect number that arrives after the decision.**
